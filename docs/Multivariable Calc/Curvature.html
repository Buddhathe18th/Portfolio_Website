Curvature

Given a vector-valued function that outputs a smooth curve, we can image a car driving along this curve. Imagine at a certain point, the steering wheel locks, and the driver starts to drive in a circle. If the point on the circle was a tight turn, then the circle the driver would turn in would have a very small radius, but if the curve is very straight, the circle would have a very large radius. If the curve was just a line, then the car wouldn't drive in a circle either way,

The concept of curvature is a way to quantify how much a curve will curve. More rigurously, we're trying to find the radius of the circle that hugs the circle the closest. We'll denote the curvature as \(\kappa\), pronounced kappa. \(\kappa\) will be the 1 over the radius of the curvature. This is intuitive since when the radius of the circle is very large, we're curving less, and when the radius is close to 0, we're turning a lot more. Numerically, the curvature is defined as \(\kappa = \lVert \dfrac{dT}{ds} \lVert\) where \(T\) is the unit tangent vector and \(s\) is the arc length. This is because we want to see how much change in the direction of the tangent for a small change in the arc length. Since we are looking at the change of the unit tangent vector, the only change is the direction change. Now let's go through the steps of computing the curvature.

Let's try to find the curvature for a function \(\overrightarrow{v}\left(t\right)=\begin{bmatrix}2t-2\sin\left(t\right)\\5-\cos^2\left(t\right)\end{bmatrix}\) at \(t=\frac{\pi}{3}\)

Now, before we start, we first should take a look at the formula for the curvature. We see we have to differentiate \(\overrightarrow{T}\) with respect to the arc length. How can we find this? The unit tangent vector isn't defined or related to the arc length at all. Therefore, what we can do is to differentiate both the top and the bottom of the fraction by \(dt\). This is because we can easily find \(\frac{\lVert\frac{d\overrightarrow{T}}{dt}\lVert}{\lVert\frac{ds}{dt}\lVert}\).

Finding \(\frac{d\overrightarrow{v}}{dt}\)

This should be quite simple since the derivative of a vector-valued function is the derivative of each component put into one vector. Therefore, \(\frac{d\overrightarrow{v}}{dt}=\begin{bmatrix}2-2\cos\left(t\right)\\2\cos\left(t\right)\sin\left(t\right)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\end{bmatrix}\). Therefore, the length of this vector is \(\sqrt{1^2+\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2}=\frac{\sqrt{7}}{2}\)

Finding \(\frac{d\overrightarrow{T}}{dt}\)

At any given \(t\), the derivative of \(\overrightarrow{v}\left(t\right)\) is equal to \(\begin{bmatrix}2-2\cos\left(t\right)\\2\cos\left(t\right)\sin\left(t\right)\end{bmatrix}\). Since by definition, the derivative is the slope of the tangent line, this is the tangent vector at point. Therefore, \(\overrightarrow{T}=\begin{bmatrix}2-2\cos\left(t\right)\\2\cos\left(t\right)\sin\left(t\right)\end{bmatrix}\). Now, we just have to normalize this, by divising by the length of the vector $$\small{\lVert\overrightarrow{T}\lVert=\sqrt{\left(2-2\cos\left(t\right)\right)^2+\left(2\cos\left(t\right)\sin\left(t\right)\right)^2}=\sqrt{4 - 8 \cos\left(t\right) + 8 \cos^2\left(t\right) - 4 \cos^4\left(t\right)}}$$ Now, our unit tangent vector is \(\overrightarrow{T}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2-2\cos\left(t\right)}{\sqrt{4 - 8 \cos\left(t\right) + 8 \cos^2\left(t\right) - 4 \cos^4\left(t\right)}}\\\frac{2\cos\left(t\right)\sin\left(t\right)}{\sqrt{4 - 8 \cos\left(t\right) + 8 \cos^2\left(t\right) - 4 \cos^4\left(t\right)}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1-\cos\left(t\right)}{\sqrt{1 - 2 \cos\left(t\right) + 2 \cos^2\left(t\right) - \cos^4\left(t\right)}}\\\frac{\cos\left(t\right)\sin\left(t\right)}{\sqrt{1 - 2 \cos\left(t\right) + 2 \cos^2\left(t\right) - \cos^4\left(t\right)}}\end{bmatrix}\)


Now, we want to find \(\frac{d\overrightarrow{T}}{dt}\) so we differentiate again, giving $$\frac{d\overrightarrow{T}}{dt}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sin\left(t\right)\cos\left(t\right) \left(\cos\left(t\right) - 1\right)\left(\cos\left(t\right)^2 - \cos\left(t\right) - 1\right)}{{\sqrt{1 - 2 \cos\left(t\right) + 2 \cos^2\left(t\right) - \cos^4\left(t\right)}^3}}\\\frac{2\sin^4\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)\left(-2\cos\left(t\right)+\cos\left(2t\right)-1\right)}{{\sqrt{1 - 2 \cos\left(t\right) + 2 \cos^2\left(t\right) - \cos^4\left(t\right)}}^3}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\frac{5\sqrt3}{32}}{\frac{7\sqrt{7}}{64}}\\\frac{-\frac{5}{16}}{\frac{7\sqrt{7}}{64}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{10\sqrt{21}}{49}\\\frac{-20\sqrt7}{49}\end{bmatrix}$$

$$\lVert\dfrac{d\overrightarrow{T}}{dt}\lVert=\sqrt{\left(\frac{10\sqrt{21}}{49}\right)^2+\left(\frac{-20\sqrt7}{49}\right)^2}=\frac{10}{7}$$ so the curvature \(\kappa=\frac{\lVert\frac{d\overrightarrow{T}}{dt}\lVert}{\lVert\frac{ds}{dt}\lVert}=\frac{\frac{10}{7}}{\frac{\sqrt7}{2}}=\frac{20\sqrt7}{49}\)

We can verify this answer by graphing this curve along with this circle.

Applications in higher dimensions

With more inputs, the function \(f\) would have a larger number of "dimensions" when graphed, but the curvature is calculated in the same way. This is because the curvature is a scalar value, equal to the radius of the circle that hugs the curve the best at that point.
